1. From face capture to useful measurements. · 0:20
My goal today is to share implementation lessons you can use in your own apps. The practical challenge is getting from a camera image to a facial dimension that is meaningful for respirator selection. I will connect capture choices to our perimeter-based fit model, show the ranking results, and outline the Android pipeline.

4. Keep the nose path separate from total perimeter. · 1:05
Previously, nose, upper cheek, mid-cheek and chin paths were summed into a single perimeter proxy. Two people with the same total could receive the same score despite different nose shapes. The new model retains the total-perimeter term and adds a separate squared nose mismatch. The face nose path is an input; the mask and style preferred nose distance is inferred from fit labels, not measured mask geometry. Across three initializations on the same five user-grouped folds, precision rose from 56.4% to 63.9%, recall rose from 58.0% to 59.4%, and log loss fell from 1.108 to 0.931. Precision asks: among tests called fitting, how many had positive labels? Recall asks: among positive labels, how many were found? Log loss penalizes confident mistakes; lower is better. All classification metrics use the fixed 50 percent cutoff except log loss, which uses the scores directly. These are averages of pooled held-out metrics from each initialization, not training scores or independent replicated datasets. The mixed cohort contains measured and imputed ARKit paths. The regression used for imputation was fitted before CV, so full-pipeline generalization is not independently established. Measured-only training on 615 tests from 18 people did not reproduce the gain: average F1 fell from .620 to .578. Calibration remains poor: even the new log loss is worse than the prevalence-only baseline of approximately .588. Labels mix evidence sources and repeated user-mask tests can remain. This is exploratory internal evidence. Shared nose sensitivity is now the training default in code; style-specific sensitivity remains experimental.

5. A stronger shortlist, with a Top-1 tradeoff. · 1:05
This compares the same two models and the same mixed-input dataset as the preceding slide. Each rate is the unweighted mean across five user-grouped folds, then averaged across three initializations. Top one changes from 54.2% to 53.0%, top three from 79.5% to 81.3%, and top five from 82.0% to 85.2%. Top three means at least one of the first three ranked held-out observations had a positive fit label. The denominator includes 32 people with at least one positive held-out label; eligible fold counts were 5, 4, 5, 8 and 10. Candidates are their existing test observations, not the entire catalog; repeated user-mask tests can remain. These rates are conditional ranking relevance, not the chance that sending three new masks to any new person succeeds. The three initializations share the same folds and are not confidence intervals. Measured-only training does not show the same gains: top one fell from 91.7 to 87.5 percent, top three from 95.8 to 93.1 percent, and top five remained 95.8 percent. That smaller cohort has a different population and test mix, so its absolute rates should not be compared with the mixed cohort.

6. The tradeoff: metric scale, speed, and reach. · 1:00
These are three different starting points. In our TrueDepth-based ARKit workflow, the mesh has metric coordinates, so I can calculate a distance in meters and convert to millimeters. MediaPipe is very useful for real-time landmark detection across camera platforms, but its standard output is normalized image coordinates and relative depth. Converting normalized coordinates to pixels does not tell me the physical size of the face. Google also offers a Face Geometry representation with canonical metric units; that is not an independent measurement of the particular person’s absolute size. Photogrammetry reconstructs a surface from multiple images. It supports ordinary cameras, but still needs physical scale and anatomical landmarks. The roughly 45 seconds here is an earlier report for raw reconstruction, not a benchmark of the updated capture, reconstruction, smoothing and landmarking workflow.

7. ARKit vs. caliper: an exploratory self-check. · 0:40
ARKit simplified capture because it gave me metric coordinates in real time, without a separate pixel-to-millimeter conversion. I also compared six ARKit-derived dimensions of my own face with digital caliper measurements. The absolute differences ranged from 2 to 13 millimeters, or 3.6 to 20 percent of the caliper values. Nose breadth had the largest relative difference, 20 percent. This is an exploratory one-person comparison, not an accuracy study or a reference standard. Landmark definitions, caliper technique, device behavior, and repeated-capture variability still need to be evaluated. The published 2023 ARKit study measured eye-to-phone distance at different angles and reported errors from 0.88 to 9.07 percent under its tested conditions. It did not test distances between facial landmarks or validate MasqFit’s nose, cheek, and chin paths.

8. A mesh vertex is not an anatomical definition. · 0:40
This screenshot shows the MediaPipe mesh running in a MasqFit web prototype. A mesh vertex is not automatically the anatomical point we want. Both systems fit or predict facial geometry, so a returned coordinate may be inferred even where the surface is not visible from that angle. That is useful for tracking, but I have not established the accuracy of these hidden points from a single view. We should compare visible and occluded landmarks against an independent reference, across head angles and single-view versus multi-view capture. More frames from one angle do not necessarily reveal the hidden surface. Multi-view capture adds observations, but its benefit still needs testing.

9. Photos now produce a physically scaled digital headform. · 0:40
Since the October draft, the camera-only path has become a working local prototype. This is an actual reconstructed head, not a fitted FLAME shape. The same 61-photo capture includes the jaw and upper neck. Gaussian volume smoothing reduces graininess while source-photo checks remain available. A measured bitragion–subnasale tape arc sets physical scale; it is not inferred from an average model face. This is a digital headform, not a fabricated or uniformly validated surface. Smoothing does not establish anatomy where source coverage is missing. The complete updated workflow has not been benchmarked for speed or validated on Android.

10. Landmarks support chords and surface routes. · 0:40
The prototype now proposes points using existing dense multi-photo correspondences, triangulates them with the registered cameras, and checks support on the raw surface before attaching them to the smoothed one. Source-photo overlays let us inspect whether the coordinates match the anatomical contact intended. The presenter visually approved the proposed nasal-wing and cheekbone contacts for this capture. Straight widths use Euclidean 3D endpoint chords. Surface routes use local triangle-strip shortcuts through controls; these are not exact global geodesics or physical tape simulations. The upper-neck coverage supports inspection of under-menton routes for trifold and duckbill styles. These points are not a direct ARKit-to-FLAME equivalence claim, and the full perimeter is still exploratory.

11. Three linear checks agree within 2.3 mm in one pilot. · 0:40
The scale anchor is the median of three bitragion–subnasale tape readings: 288, 280 and 284 millimeters. With that scale fixed, the headform gives nose width 35.2 versus a 35.0 millimeter caliper median, face length 112.3 versus 114.6, and face width 153.0 versus 150.9. These are separate dimensions from the calibration arc, with repeated caliper readings but only one reconstruction capture. The endpoints and methods were inspected during development, so these are initial agreement checks, not a blinded validation set. Next steps are independent repeated captures, predefined landmarks and reference protocols, and more people, devices and lighting conditions. Perimeter work remains separate: a future experiment may photograph a suitable bifold mask while worn, reconstruct/register it and trace its contact arc. The exploratory perimeter discrepancy is omitted from this presentation.

14. A complementary scope for respirator research. · 0:30
NIOSH’s published face-measuring app description focuses on facial dimensions and fit-panel classification. MasqFit and Breathesafe can complement that work through a broader mask catalog, child-inclusive contributed data, and open GPLv3 code. Our catalog includes KN95 and FFP2 or FFP3 options as well as NIOSH-approved respirators. The dataset includes children and child-sized masks, but pediatric prediction accuracy has not yet been established. Open code allows method inspection and reproduction where data access permits; participant data require appropriate consent and privacy controls. These are scope differences, not a head-to-head accuracy claim or a statement about the final features of NIOSH’s apps.

16. Synthetic faces remain a complementary research path. · Backup
This is complementary research, separate from the working multi-photo correspondence prototype shown in the main talk. Begin with a 3D face, either synthetic or from an actual captured person. Use rendered or captured 2D views to detect the target landmarks, then project those detections back onto the matching 3D surface. A planned CNN detector would learn local visual patterns around features such as eyes, nose, ears, and other landmarks in those 2D views. The screenshot shows hand-labeled landmarks that can support training and evaluation; it does not show a trained CNN result. Projection requires the camera-to-surface relationship: for a rendered view it is known; for a captured view it must be recovered or calibrated. A point on the image does not select the correct surface point without that relationship and visibility checks. Once the landmarks are on a scaled surface, calculate the defined dimensions and pass them into the fit-prediction pipeline. These screenshots show the current FLAME synthetic-face generator and anatomical landmark labeling tool. They are research tools for the Android path, not Android app screens. Synthetic models let us generate known geometry labels and controlled views. They do not tell us whether a respirator fits a real person. The difficult transfer is from synthetic rendering to real cameras and faces, which needs independent evaluation.

17. The prototype uses one physical scale anchor. · Backup
Use this only if asked about implementation. Rendered geometry provides known cameras for development. Captured geometry requires estimated or calibrated cameras. A known reference dimension must be independent of the target used for accuracy evaluation; otherwise calibration can conceal the error being tested.

18. Sources and boundaries of the claims. · Backup
Prototype evidence updated November 11, 2026; source hashes retained in the frozen pilot summary. Technical references checked October 29, 2026. Recommender comparison results come from the November 12 paired local experiment, with frozen aggregates and source hashes in recommender-evidence.json. The personal recommendation screenshot remains the October 25 model. They are internal retrospective ranking results, not a prospective evaluation. Model architecture is traced to python/mask_recommender/train.py, especially calc_preds and _resolve_custom_lr_parameter_views. For fixed family, style, and strap type, A = a_family + a_style < 0, B = b_family + b_style, C = c_family + c_style + s_strap. The centered equation uses kappa = -A, delta_star = -B/(2A), and c = C - B²/(4A). Legacy parameter files can bypass the newer negative-curvature parameterization; the historical dashboard snapshot does not independently establish its saved parameter constraints. Mixed labels and preprocessing/imputation also need an end-to-end validation audit.
