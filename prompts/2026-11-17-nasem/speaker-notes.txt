1. From face capture to useful measurements. · 0:20
My goal today is to share implementation lessons you can use in your own apps. The practical challenge is getting from a camera image to a facial dimension that is meaningful for respirator selection. I will connect capture choices to our perimeter-based fit model, show the ranking results, and outline the Android pipeline.

3. A preferred perimeter difference, learned from outcomes. · 1:15
The face measurement enters the recommender as a perimeter proxy: four selected facial paths added together. Subtract the mask perimeter and convert millimeters to centimeters. This is a signed difference of lengths, not a physical gap between the face and mask. A quadratic in that difference feeds a logistic function. In this centered form, delta star is the preferred difference, kappa controls how quickly the score falls away, and c sets the peak logit. The optimum need not be zero: the model can learn that a family and style work best at a nonzero difference. Family and style supply the quadratic coefficients; strap type supplies an offset. New training constrains curvature to be negative. The plotted curves use invented coefficients to explain the shape; they are not measured mask response curves. The sigmoid gives a probability-like fit score for ranking, but calibration needs separate evaluation. A limitation is geometry compression: for the same mask, two faces with the same summed perimeter get the same score.

4. How often was a fitting result near the top? · 1:15
These are the saved September dashboard results: top one 64.2 percent, top three 90.3 percent, and top five 92.8 percent. The question is whether at least one positive fit label appears among the first k ranked held-out test observations. The reported percentages are unweighted means across five user-grouped folds. Only users with a positive held-out result contribute. The candidate set is their observed test rows, not the entire catalog, and repeated observations can remain. For example, top three is a hit if one of the first three ranked observations has a positive label. It is not a claim that sending three new masks to an arbitrary new user will yield a 90.3 percent chance of a fit. The labels combine formal test sources and constructed outcomes, so this is internal relevance evidence rather than a formal-test-only prospective trial. Fold counts and uncertainty were not exposed by this snapshot. The transferable lesson is to evaluate ranking relevance explicitly, then test the user decision prospectively.

5. The tradeoff: metric scale, speed, and reach. · 1:00
These are three different starting points. In our TrueDepth-based ARKit workflow, the mesh has metric coordinates, so I can calculate a distance in meters and convert to millimeters. MediaPipe is very useful for real-time landmark detection across camera platforms, but its standard output is normalized image coordinates and relative depth. Converting normalized coordinates to pixels does not tell me the physical size of the face. Google also offers a Face Geometry representation with canonical metric units; that is not an independent measurement of the particular person’s absolute size. Photogrammetry reconstructs a surface from multiple images. It supports ordinary cameras, but still needs physical scale and anatomical landmarks. The roughly 45 seconds here is my reported processing experience, not a benchmark across implementations, devices, capture time, or uploads.

6. ARKit made the measurement experience simpler. · 0:40
ARKit felt more seamless because I did not need to ask the user to supply a conversion between pixels and physical size. On the TrueDepth devices used by this workflow, Apple combines its camera system and infrared depth sensing with a face model. The mesh supports real-time distance calculations, which helps make the capture interaction immediate. That is an implementation advantage rather than a measurement validation result. A model can report millimeters and still be biased at a particular facial region. Also, ARKit face tracking availability is broader than TrueDepth on some newer devices; I am describing our TrueDepth measurement path, not asserting that every ARKit-compatible device has the same depth hardware or metrology performance.

7. A mesh vertex is not an anatomical definition. · 0:40
A mesh vertex is not automatically the anatomical point we want. Both systems fit or predict facial geometry, so a returned coordinate may be inferred even where the surface is not visible from that angle. That is useful for tracking, but I have not established the accuracy of these hidden points from a single view. We should compare visible and occluded landmarks against an independent reference, across head angles and single-view versus multi-view capture. More frames from one angle do not necessarily reveal the hidden surface. Multi-view capture adds observations, but its benefit still needs testing.

8. Broader camera support adds new work. · 1:00
Photogrammetry is attractive because it can use ordinary cameras rather than relying on a particular depth sensor. Published work has shown good facial anthropometric performance for specific photogrammetric systems. That is motivation to investigate, not evidence that all smartphone or webcam implementations achieve the same accuracy. The cost is a slower experience: multiple views and reconstruction rather than an immediate mesh. In my workflow, processing has been around 45 seconds. That changes the interface: users need capture instructions, a clear waiting state, and a useful way to recover from failure. Reconstruction itself does not provide the anatomical labels I need. Scale and landmark detection remain separate engineering and validation steps.

9. Separate reconstruction from landmarking. · 1:20
This is the pipeline I am working toward. Begin with a 3D face, either synthetic or from an actual captured person. Use rendered or captured 2D views to detect the target landmarks, then project those detections back onto the matching 3D surface. Projection requires the camera-to-surface relationship: for a rendered view it is known; for a captured view it must be recovered or calibrated. A point on the image does not select the correct surface point without that relationship and visibility checks. Once the landmarks are on a scaled surface, calculate the defined dimensions and pass them into the fit-prediction pipeline. Synthetic models let us generate known geometry labels and controlled views. They do not tell us whether a respirator fits a real person. The difficult transfer is from synthetic rendering to real cameras and faces, which needs independent evaluation.

10. Validate the handoffs, not only the final score. · 1:30
For your iOS and Android apps, I would start with a common measurement contract. Specify the landmarks, units, and distance definitions independently of the camera library. Record the capture method and version so that a change in reconstruction is traceable. Then validate the handoffs. At capture, count failures and repeated-scan variability across devices and conditions. At measurement, compare each dimension to an independent reference and report bias and repeatability, not just correlation. At prediction, hold out people and use fit-test outcomes to evaluate whether the measurements improve selection. Keep synthetic, printed-face, and real-human evidence separate. If the camera route changes a feature’s meaning or distribution, do not assume a model trained on the old route will transfer unchanged. The useful collaboration is agreeing on definitions and reference measurements before optimizing the interface.

11. Choose the capture route. Keep the measurement contract. · 0:30
My main lesson is to separate the capture technology from the scientific definition of the measurement. ARKit simplified the first iOS experience. Moving toward Android means making scale, landmark placement, and reconstruction explicit. I would welcome discussion about a shared set of anatomical definitions and an independent reference protocol that could help both projects. Thank you.

12. The camera-only path needs a scale anchor. · Backup
Use this only if asked about implementation. Rendered geometry provides known cameras for development. Captured geometry requires estimated or calibrated cameras. A known reference dimension must be independent of the target used for accuracy evaluation; otherwise calibration can conceal the error being tested.

13. Sources and boundaries of the claims. · Backup
Technical references checked October 29, 2026. Recommender results come from the frozen September 24 dashboard, model timestamp September 20, 2026. They are historical internal ranking results, not a fresh evaluation. Model architecture is traced to python/mask_recommender/train.py, especially calc_preds and _resolve_custom_lr_parameter_views. For fixed family, style, and strap type, A = a_family + a_style < 0, B = b_family + b_style, C = c_family + c_style + s_strap. The centered equation uses kappa = -A, delta_star = -B/(2A), and c = C - B²/(4A). Legacy parameter files can bypass the newer negative-curvature parameterization; the historical dashboard snapshot does not independently establish its saved parameter constraints. Mixed labels and preprocessing/imputation also need an end-to-end validation audit.
